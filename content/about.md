# About Pardonpedia

Pardonpedia is a structured reference database of U.S. presidential pardons and clemency grants, covering Nixon through the present day. The project is actively maintained — new clemency actions are added as they occur and historical records are continuously expanded.

For more detail, see [Pardon Data Sources](pardon-data-sources.html), [Pardon Patterns](pardon-patterns.html), [Data Dictionary](data-dictionary.html), and [Release Notes](release-notes.html).

## About the Pardon Power

The President's clemency power is established in Article II, Section 2, Clause 1 of the U.S. Constitution, which grants the President the power to "grant Reprieves and Pardons for Offences against the United States, except in Cases of Impeachment."

Several features of this constitutional grant are significant:

**Federal offenses only.** The phrase "Offences against the United States" limits the pardon power strictly to federal crimes. The President cannot pardon a person for a state-level offense. State governors hold equivalent clemency authority within their own jurisdictions under their respective state constitutions.

**No pardons in impeachment cases.** The explicit exception for impeachment means the President cannot use the pardon power to shield an official from an impeachment proceeding or its consequences.

**Broad and largely unreviewable.** The Supreme Court has consistently held that the pardon power is broad and that its exercise is largely unreviewable by Congress or the courts. In *Ex parte Garland* (1866), the Court described the power as "unlimited" except as specified in the Constitution itself. In *Schick v. Reed* (1974), the Court upheld the power to attach conditions to a pardon. There is ongoing constitutional debate about whether a president may pardon themselves — a question the courts have not resolved.

**No requirement of conviction.** The pardon power may be exercised before a conviction, before an indictment, or even before any formal charge has been filed. Such grants are called preemptive or anticipatory pardons. The most prominent historical example is President Ford's pardon of Richard Nixon in 1974, issued before Nixon was charged with any crime.

## Pardon Terminology

**Clemency** is the umbrella term for the various forms of executive relief from criminal punishment. When a president grants clemency, they are exercising the pardon power in one of the specific forms below. All of these apply exclusively to federal offenses.

| Term | Affects conviction? | Affects prison sentence? | Affects financial penalties? | Applies to groups? | Notes |
|------|--------------------|--------------------------|-----------------------------|-------------------|-------|
| Pardon | Yes — forgives it | Yes — eliminates it | Partially — see below | No (see Amnesty) | Restores civil rights; does not expunge record |
| Commutation | No | Yes — reduces or eliminates it | Sometimes — if explicitly included | No | Conviction stands; only punishment is reduced |
| Reprieve | No | No — delays only | No | No | Temporary; sentence still applies |
| Remission | No | No | Yes — reduces or eliminates fines | No | Applies to fines only; does not affect prison term |
| Amnesty | Yes — forgives it | Yes — eliminates it | Partially — see below | Yes | Blanket grant to a class of people |
| Fine | N/A | N/A | Monetary penalty paid to the government | N/A | Imposed as part of criminal sentence |
| Restitution | N/A | N/A | Payment to compensate victims | N/A | Survives a pardon in most circumstances — see below |
| Forfeiture | N/A | N/A | Seizure of assets connected to the crime | N/A | May be criminal or civil — see below |

### Financial Penalties

Criminal sentences frequently include financial obligations in addition to — or instead of — incarceration. Pardonpedia records these where they appear in court documents.

- **Fine** — A monetary penalty paid to the federal government, imposed by the court as part of a criminal sentence. Fines are punitive in nature and flow to the government, not to any victim.

- **Restitution** — A payment ordered by the court to compensate identifiable victims for losses directly caused by the crime. Restitution is distinct from a fine: it is owed to specific people or entities, not to the government. Restitution obligations are frequently large in fraud, theft, and financial crime cases.

- **Forfeiture** — The surrender of assets connected to the crime. Criminal forfeiture is imposed as part of a sentence and covers proceeds of the crime and property used to commit it. Civil forfeiture is a separate legal proceeding against the property itself and does not require a criminal conviction. Both forms may appear in a defendant's case record.

### How Clemency Affects Financial Penalties

The relationship between clemency and financial penalties is nuanced and frequently misunderstood.

**Pardons and fines.** A presidential pardon generally eliminates any unpaid fine that was imposed as part of the sentence. The specific mechanism for reducing or eliminating a fine is a remission, but a full pardon is broadly understood to cover it.

**Pardons and restitution.** A presidential pardon does not automatically eliminate a restitution obligation. Because restitution is treated as a remedy for victims — not purely as punishment — courts have held that it survives a pardon in most circumstances. Victims retain the right to pursue collection of unpaid restitution even after a pardon is granted. This is one of the most practically significant limitations of the pardon power for defendants in financial crime cases.

**Pardons and forfeiture.** Criminal forfeiture, imposed as part of a sentence, may be affected by a pardon, though the law in this area is not fully settled. Civil forfeiture, because it is a proceeding against property rather than a criminal punishment, is generally not affected by a presidential pardon at all. Assets already forfeited and transferred to the government are not automatically returned upon a pardon.

**Commutations and financial penalties.** A commutation reduces or eliminates a prison sentence but does not automatically affect fines, restitution, or forfeiture unless the commutation order explicitly says so.
